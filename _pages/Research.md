## Working Papers

**The Costs of Decreased Financial Statement Comparability: Evidence from Divergent Leasing Standards**  
with Jeremy Michels and Rachel Zhang  
*Accepted for the 2026 Review of Accounting Studies Conference*

Contemporaneous changes to the accounting for leases under IFRS and US GAAP resulted in less comparable income statement presentation of lease-related expenses across the two reporting regimes. In this study, we examine how this decrease in comparability is associated with information processing costs for financial statement users. We examine the forecasting behavior of analysts who follow firms that report under both IFRS and US GAAP and thus are most affected by the reduced comparability across the standards. We find that these analysts, relative to those who follow firms reporting under a single lease accounting standard, are less likely to forecast EBITDA and exhibit longer EBITDA-specific forecast delays. We also differentiate between time-series and cross-sectional comparability and find that our results appear driven by reduced cross-sectional comparability. Overall, our evidence suggests that the decrease in income statement comparability resulting from the new leasing standards significantly increases information processing costs, even among relatively sophisticated market participants.

**When Credit Unions Acquire Community Banks**  
with Laurel Mazur

The acquisition of community banks by credit unions has garnered significant attention in recent years. One reason for this is the fact that, once the acquisition is completed, the combined entity is no longer subject to Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) oversight, the only federal regulation requiring banks to serve low-and-moderate-income (LMI) areas and borrowers. Using loan-level HMDA data covering acquisitions completed from 2016 to 2024, we find a decline in the likelihood of mortgage approval. We further find that this decline is more pronounced for low-income borrowers, rather than specifically in CRA-designated LMI areas. This pattern is consistent with a reduction in the supply of credit that disproportionately affects income-constrained borrowers, possibly arising from changes in underwriting or a loss of relationship-lending capacity. Overall, the study finds some evidence that these acquisitions serve to curtail the supply of credit to LMI borrowers, suggesting a potential role for CRA oversight.

**Does the Credit Union Tax Exemption Distort Competition in Local Lending Markets?**  
with Jennifer Blouin

We examine whether the credit union tax exemption distorts competition in local mortgage markets, using the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act as a shock that narrowed the tax advantage of credit unions relative to C-corporation banks. After establishing that C-corp banks reduced mortgage rates by approximately 15 basis points following the TCJA, we find that credit unions in counties with greater C-corp bank presence increased originations by 8 to 11% while compressing average loan sizes by approximately 3%. This response is concentrated in counties where open-charter credit unions have meaningful market presence and is absent in narrow-charter counties, consistent with competitive flexibility driving the effect. The results suggest that tax policy shapes local mortgage market competition through the credit union tax exemption, with credit unions expanding access to smaller borrowers when faced with pricing pressure from banks.

**Voluntary Sustainability Reporting Expands Firm Disclosure but Rarely Changes Behavior**  
with Shawn Kim and Panos Patatoukas

Adoption of voluntary sustainability reporting frameworks has surged globally, yet whether they generate meaningful changes in corporate behavior remains unclear. Here, we examine the outcomes of US firms' adoption of the SASB Standards, one of the most widely used voluntary sustainability reporting frameworks globally, to provide a direct test of the framework that underpins global sustainability reporting standards. Comparing adopters to matched non-adopters across eight outcome variables spanning ESG and environmental performance, capital market liquidity, and corporate investment, we find that SASB adoption is associated with increases in sustainability disclosure scores, stated environmental initiatives, and ESG scores, and a modest reduction in bid-ask spreads. However, we find limited improvements in outcomes such as emissions and no improvement in price impact or investment activity. Event-study analyses reveal that much of the disclosure increase reflects pre-existing upward trends among firms that subsequently adopt SASB, rather than a causal effect of adoption. Triple-difference designs comparing full versus partial adopters and carbon-intensive versus less carbon-intensive industries reveal no separation by depth of commitment, contrary to costly signaling, and a single substantive exception: emissions intensity declines, concentrated in the most carbon-intensive industries. These findings suggest that voluntary sustainability standards, absent verification and enforcement mechanisms, may largely expand reported transparency without changing underlying corporate behavior.

**The Sound of Whose Voice? Comment Letter Influence at the FASB**  
with Rose Hartnett and Cassie Mongold

The FASB's most visible channel for stakeholder input is the comment letter process, yet little large-sample evidence exists on whether this process differentially accommodates certain stakeholder groups. We examine how stakeholder advocacy relates to the Board's decisions to revise or maintain its proposed positions between the exposure draft and the final standard, using an LLM-assisted pipeline to extract positions from comment letters and match them to specific Board decisions across 13 Accounting Standards Updates issued between 2022 and 2025. We find that investor opposition to a proposed provision is associated with a lower probability that the Board revises its position, while Big 4 audit firm opposition is associated with a substantially higher probability of revision. This asymmetry is concentrated on lower-salience issues that attract fewer comment letters; on high-attention items, the investor disadvantage largely disappears. The pattern suggests that the many lower-profile decisions that cumulatively shape a standard are disproportionately responsive to implementation-side stakeholders.

**Opening the Black Box of CECL: Quantitative Models and Qualitative Adjustments**  
with Brad Badertscher and Laurel Mazur

The Current Expected Credit Loss (CECL) standard allows for significant discretion in implementation, primarily through the qualitative adjustment. Despite its importance, the qualitative adjustment is largely unexplored because researchers cannot distinguish it from the quantitative allowance. We overcome this limitation by exploiting a unique setting in the credit union sector: the National Credit Union Administration's publicly available Simplified CECL Tool. We replicate the tool's model-implied quantitative allowance and estimate the qualitative adjustment as the difference between the reported allowance and this benchmark. We find that the qualitative adjustment predicts future net charge-offs at both the four- and eight-quarter horizons, incremental to the quantitative estimate. Decomposing the qualitative adjustment into a component predictable from observable economic indicators and an orthogonal residual, both components explain losses in the short term, while only the observable economic component is informative at longer horizons. The predictive content is concentrated among more mature credit unions and those more likely to create and process more soft information about their member base. Overall, our findings show that the qualitative adjustment under CECL contains information rather than discretionary noise.

**The Calibration of Cost Claims in Accounting Standard Setting**  
with Cassie Mongold

Accounting standard setters are directed to weigh expected implementation costs against expected benefits, yet their primary window into those costs is unverifiable claims from the parties with a direct stake in the standard's fate. We examine whether these claims contain information about the costs firms subsequently realize. Using a large language model pipeline, we convert 9,395 comment letters on FASB exposure drafts into standard-level measures of claimed cost intensity and link them to adoption events identified from 10-K disclosures for a panel of 51,824 firm-years from 2012 through 2024. We find claim intensity predicts realized costs: firms adopting standards claimed to be more costly pay approximately 0.9 percent higher audit fees per standard deviation of claimed intensity in the adoption year, and their SG&A rises beginning the year before the effective date. The response declines monotonically with firm size, is roughly three times larger and persistent for the smallest adopters, and concentrates in the standards whose letters claim disproportionate small-entity burden. Claims fail on one dimension: although two-thirds characterize costs as ongoing, realized cost increases appear transitory. Our results suggest that comment letters are a usable input to cost-benefit analysis when read for intensity and incidence rather than at face value.

**Local Deposit Competition and Depositor Discipline: Evidence from Credit Union Expansions**  
with Rui Shi and Detian Yang

We examine how local deposit competition affects depositor discipline. Our setting exploits a regulatory change that allowed U.S. credit unions with "Low-Income Credit Union" (LICU) designation to relax field-of-membership restrictions and compete directly with nearby banks for retail deposits. Using a difference-in-differences design at the bank–market level, we study how LICU-induced competition changes the sensitivity of deposit flows to bank-specific performance. We focus on two dimensions of information: "hard" financial performance (ROA) and "soft" information about service quality (consumer complaints in CFPB data). We find that, prior to LICU entry, deposit flows are only weakly related to either ROA or complaints. Following LICU expansion, deposit flows become significantly more sensitive to both, with a one–standard-deviation decline in ROA associated with substantially larger deposit outflows in exposed markets. The effect is stronger in markets with multiple LICU entrants and higher financial literacy, and is not explained by changes in deposit rates. Banks facing greater outflows respond by improving complaint resolution and adjusting deposit pricing. Overall, our evidence shows that deposit competition is an important moderator of depositor discipline.
